# Cultural and intellectual property

**In short:** your community's rights in its language and knowledge stay with your
community, whatever the copyright says. The kit gives you ways to keep control of your
material, take it back, credit the people it came from, and decide where it goes.

This is the design. It isn't built yet.

## What ICIP means here

Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property (ICIP) is a community's rights in its
language, knowledge, stories, songs and other cultural expression. These rights:

- belong to the community as a whole, not to whoever recorded or published the material,
- don't run out the way copyright does, and
- aren't fully protected by Australian law, so communities and organisations use
  agreed protocols to respect them.

An old grammar or word list may be out of copyright and still carry your community's
rights. The kit treats it that way.

lect-kit itself is released under the MIT licence. That covers the kit's code and these
pages. It gives nobody any rights in a community's language. The [LICENSE](../LICENSE)
file includes an ICIP notice that says so.

## Where this comes from

The kit's approach draws on published practice, including:

- Terri Janke's True Tracks principles for ICIP,
- the AIATSIS Code of Ethics for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander research,
- the CARE principles for Indigenous data governance, and
- Indigenous data sovereignty principles, such as those of Maiam nayri Wingara.

These are guides, and your community's own protocols come first.

## What the kit lets you do

**Take material back.** You can withdraw any material at any time. The kit rebuilds
everything without it, including tables, practice and search. If you've shared it with
another community in an exchange package, the kit prepares a note asking them to remove
it too.

**Credit the people it came from.** Everything the kit produces can acknowledge your
community and the knowledge holders behind it, in the words you choose.

**Attach your own labels.** You can attach your community's protocols to material, for
example using Traditional Knowledge Labels and Notices from Local Contexts. They travel
with the material and show wherever it appears.

**Decide where your data goes.** The kit can run entirely on your own computers, and
without AI at all. [Using AI, or not](ai.md) explains how to keep your knowledge out of
AI training if you do use AI. By
default it sends nothing to an outside service, an AI service included. If your
community decides some material may be processed elsewhere, you say which material and
where.

**Set terms on what you publish.** Pages the kit produces can carry your terms of use,
including whether the material may be used to train AI. Unless you decide otherwise,
everything the kit publishes says it may not, and carries every signal the kit knows
of in the pages, in the files and in the website settings it produces. [Using AI, or
not](ai.md) lists them. A notice won't stop everyone who ignores it, and the kit
doesn't pretend otherwise. You might also choose a licence
written for community data, such as the Kaitiakitanga License that Te Hiku Media wrote
for Māori language data.

**Respect people who have passed away.** You can withhold the name, voice or image of
someone who has passed away, for a set time or for good, without changing anything
else about the material.

**Ask for review before publishing.** You can turn on a step where people your
community trusts look over material before it's published, such as new sentences built
for learners.

**Keep the benefit.** You can export everything you've put in, in open formats, at any
time. The kit is built for your community to use your material, not for others to take
it.

## What the kit can't do

The kit can't make anyone respect your rights. Once something is published, people can
copy it. The safest choice for sensitive material is not to publish it at all, and the
kit makes that the default for anything restricted.
